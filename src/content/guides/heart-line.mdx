### Where does your heart line start and end?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="heart-end-index" title="Heart line ending under the index finger" gist="Loyal, steady affection held to high ideals." check="Does your top line curve up and stop under your first finger?" alt="A heart line curving up to end below the index finger" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927', 'benham-laws-of-scientific-hand-reading-1900', 'cheiro-guide-to-the-hand-1900']}>

The books read this as loyal, steady affection held to high ideals: once you are truly attached, you tend to stay attached. Some also read an idealistic view of love, a habit of looking up to the person you love.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-end-between" title="Heart line ending between the index and middle fingers" gist="Calm but deep caring: not showy, but willing to do a great deal." check="Does your top line stop in the gap between your first and middle fingers?" alt="A heart line ending in the gap between the index and middle fingers" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927', 'raphael-cheirosophy-1901', 'benham-laws-of-scientific-hand-reading-1900']}>

A calm but deep way of caring. Not showy in love, but willing to do a great deal for the people you care about. Benham adds sensible, practical affection that isn’t easily swept away.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-end-middle" title="Heart line ending under the middle finger" gist="Affection kept private, shown through closeness more than words." check="Does your top line curve up and stop under your middle finger?" alt="A heart line curving up to end below the middle finger" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'cheiro-guide-to-the-hand-1900', 'desbarrolles-chiromancie-nouvelle-1859']}>

Affection that stays private and is not often put on display, with a clear sense of what you want from a relationship. It is often shown through closeness and presence as much as words. The old books judge this ending harshly; we keep only the part about a style of affection.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-straight" title="Straight heart line" gist="Read by where it ends, not by the missing curve." check="Does your top line run fairly straight, without curving up at the end?" alt="A heart line running straight across the palm without curving up" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900']}>

Most books don’t read the curve on its own. They read where the line ends. A straight line usually runs across towards the index-finger edge, so it is read like a long line; a line that curves up usually ends under a finger, so it is read by that ending.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-curved" title="Curved heart line" gist="Read by where the curve ends: under the index or the middle finger." check="Does your top line bend up steeply towards your fingers at its end?" alt="A heart line that bends up steeply towards the gap between the index and middle fingers" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900']}>

The classical books give the curve itself no separate meaning. A curved heart line is read by where the curve ends: under the index finger, between the index and middle fingers, or under the middle finger, as in the cards above. Cheiro attaches a gloomy claim about love to one rare curve, a line that bends down at the base of the index finger; it is a claim about events, so we leave it out.

</Variation>
</Variations>

### How long is your heart line?

<CompareRow framed forms={[['heart-long', 'Long'], ['heart-short', 'Short']]} />

<Variations>
<Variation variant="heart-long" title="Long heart line" gist="Wholehearted affection; running right across, it can tip into possessiveness." check="Does your top line run almost from one edge of your palm to the other?" alt="A long heart line running almost across the whole palm" src={['heron-allen-manual-of-cheirosophy-1885', 'st-hill-grammar-of-palmistry-1893', 'desbarrolles-chiromancie-nouvelle-1859', 'jain-samudrik-shastra-1927', 'dale-indian-palmistry-1895']}>

Strong, wholehearted affection: the books read a longer line as a fuller capacity for attachment. Jain’s Hindi book reads a spirited, cheerful nature, and Dale reads constancy in a long, unbroken line. When the line runs right across the hand, several books add a caution: affection given so fully can tip into possessiveness.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-short" title="Short heart line" gist="Feelings shown sparingly. Here the traditions differ. Cheiro and Frith read feelings that are shown sparingly, not always put into words or gestures. That describes how feeling is shown, not how much is felt. Dale reads attachment that is slower to hold steady.

</Variation>

### How clear is your heart line?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="heart-end-index" id="type-heart-clear" title="Deep, clearly marked heart line" gist="The books’ well-made heart line: warm, steady and even-tempered." check="Is your top line deep and clear from end to end, with no gaps?" alt="A clear, unbroken heart line ending below the index finger" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'heron-allen-manual-of-cheirosophy-1885', 'st-hill-grammar-of-palmistry-1893']}>

The form the classical books hold up as the well-made heart line, especially when it runs unbroken to the index finger: a warm, steady, affectionate nature with an even temper.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-faint" title="Faint heart line" gist="Feelings that run quietly, with a cooler manner. Not a lack of love." check="Is your top line thin and hard to see, even in daylight?" alt="A thin, faint heart line" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Feelings that run quietly under the surface, with a cooler, more detached manner in affection. It is a manner, not an absence of love. Light and focus also make lines look fainter in photos.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-chained" title="Chained heart line" gist="Attraction that forms easily and moves on easily." check="Up close, does your top line look like a row of tiny links?" alt="A heart line drawn as a row of small links" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927']}>

A chained line looks like a row of small links. The books read attraction that forms easily and moves on easily, interest in people that shifts more than it settles. It is a pattern of attraction, not a judgement of character.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-absent" title="No heart line" gist="Uncommon. A palm with no heart line at all is uncommon. More often the heart and head lines have formed one crease across the palm, the simian line (see [when the heart and head lines join](#when-the-heart-and-head-lines-join)), or the line is only faint in the photo. For a line that is truly missing, Cheiro reads a cool, unemotional manner, with feeling kept under firm control. That describes how feeling is shown, not whether a person can love.

</Variation>

### Does your heart line break, fork or branch?

<CompareRow framed forms={[['heart-fork', 'Forked'], ['heart-broken', 'Broken'], ['heart-branches-up', 'Branches rising'], ['heart-branches-down', 'Branches falling'], ['heart-double', 'Double']]} />

<Variations>
<Variation variant="heart-fork" title="Forked heart line" gist="Balanced, faithful affection: ideals and everyday warmth held together." check="Does your top line split into two small branches near its end?" alt="A heart line that splits into two branches near its end" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927', 'st-hill-grammar-of-palmistry-1893', 'jain-samudrik-shastra-1927']}>

A fork at the finger end is one of the best-liked forms in the books: a balanced, honest and faithful style of affection, with ideals and everyday warmth held together. Jain adds a cheerful, generous nature, ready to help friends. A photo can’t always show exactly where a fork sits.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-broken" title="Broken heart line" gist="Attachment that is slower to settle. It does not mean heartbreak." check="Is there a clear gap in your top line, where two pieces overlap?" alt="A heart line with a gap in the middle" src={['dale-indian-palmistry-1895', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Dale reads attachment that is slower to hold steady, and Cheiro’s general rule for broken lines is that where the two ends overlap, the line’s qualities carry on. A crease or a shadow can also make a line look broken in a photo.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-branches-up" title="Branches rising from the heart line" gist="Friendships that carry real weight in your life." check="Do small lines rise from your top line towards your fingers?" alt="A heart line with two small branches rising towards the fingers" src={['markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927']}>

Small branches rising from the line towards the fingers. Markun reads them as friendship carrying real weight: warmth shared through close, supportive friendships.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-branches-down" title="Branches falling from the heart line" gist="Feelings given to many people, which can be hurt when they aren’t returned." check="Do small lines drop from your top line towards the middle of your palm?" alt="A heart line with two small branches dropping towards the head line" src={['frith-practical-palmistry-1895', 'benham-laws-of-scientific-hand-reading-1900']}>

Small branches that drop from the heart line towards the head line. Frith reads branches below the line as disappointments in affection or friendship, and Benham’s general rule is that the more a heart line branches, the more its feeling goes out to many people. Together they describe feeling given widely, which can be hurt when it isn’t returned. That is a tendency, not a forecast of heartbreak.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="heart-double" title="Double heart line" gist="A second line close beside it: read as a capacity for deep affection." check="Does a second long line run just below your top line, close beside it?" alt="A heart line with a second line running close below it" src={['saint-germain-practice-of-palmistry-1900', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Two heart lines running close together are uncommon, and few books discuss them. Saint-Germain reads a double heart line as a capacity for deep affection, and Cheiro’s general rule is that a sister line, one running close beside another, adds to the power of the line it runs with. Saint-Germain also pairs it with future sorrow; that is a forecast, so we leave it out. Check that the second line isn’t a finger crease or your head line.

</Variation>
</Variations>

## How to tell the heart line from the head line

The heart line is the highest long line across your palm, just below your fingers. The head line sits below it. To tell them apart:

<PhotoSteps line="heart" />

1. Hold your hand up in daylight, palm facing you, fingers together.
2. Skip the short creases at the base of each finger. Those are finger creases, not palm lines.
3. The first long line below them, starting at the little-finger edge, is your heart line. It runs towards the index or middle finger, and often curves up at the end.

The next long line below it starts from the thumb side: that is the [head line](/head-line/). If you see only one long line across the top of your palm instead of two, read [when the heart and head lines join](#when-the-heart-and-head-lines-join).

<FlowChoice />

### How the heart line and head line differ

The two lines are read for different things. The heart line is read for feeling: how you show affection and relate to people. The head line is read for thinking: how you learn, plan and decide. Cheiro also reads the space between them. When the heart line sits very low, almost touching the head line, he reads feelings that keep stepping into decisions. When the head line sits unusually high, close under the heart line, he reads the reverse: matters of the heart decided by the head. That space has its own name, the [quadrangle](/head-line/#the-quadrangle-the-space-between-the-head-line-and-heart-line).

## Is the love line the same as the heart line?

Yes. The love line in your hand is the heart line: “love line” is simply another name for it. In Hindi it is <Hi>हृदय रेखा</Hi> (hriday rekha). Older English books call it the mensal line, and Mrs Dale’s *Indian Palmistry* (1895) calls it the Line of Fortune. They all mean the same top line.

The small lines on the edge of your palm, under the little finger, are something else. Palmistry books call them marriage or relationship lines, and we explain them, honestly, in the [marriage line guide](/marriage-line/).

## Which end of the heart line do you read from?

Palmistry books disagree about this. Some read the heart line from the little-finger edge towards the index finger; others start from the index-finger end. So when two books read the same heart line in different ways, a careful reading shows you both instead of quietly picking one. That is what the PalmSays app does when its sources disagree.

<KeyPoint>Most readings look at just four things: where the line ends, how long it is, how clearly it is marked, and any forks, breaks or branches.</KeyPoint>

## Marks on the heart line

The heart line can carry small marks: dots, islands, crosses, stars, squares or triangles. Books give each a meaning, but these marks are usually only a few pixels wide in a phone photo, too small to see reliably. So an honest reading leaves them out. What a good photo does show is a line’s **breaks**, **forks** and **branches**, and those are what the cards above describe.

Some Indian readings talk about signs such as a trident (<Hi>त्रिशूल</Hi>) or a fish (<Hi>मछली</Hi>) on or near the heart line. We will cover those signs, and what the books actually say about them, in a separate guide.

## Heart line in the left and right hand, for women and men

The heart line is read the same way for everyone. There is no separate heart line for women or men. What changes is **which hand** you look at:

- **Your writing hand** is read as the life you are making.
- **Your other hand** is read as what you started with.

<WhichHand />

Cheiro puts it as the right hand showing what a person has developed, and the left the natural tendencies. So compare both: if the line looks different on each hand, the tradition reads that as a change from where you started. [Which hand to read](/which-hand-to-read/) explains the traditions, including left-handed readers.

## When the heart and head lines join

Sometimes the heart line and the head line don’t run as two lines. They form one straight crease across the palm. Palmistry calls it the simian line; doctors call it a single palmar crease. It is usually a normal variation. Cheiro reads a single line like this as great intensity of character, and says where it sits matters: at head-line height it leans to the mind, higher up to feeling. The [simian line guide](/simian-line/) covers the medical facts first, then what the books say.

## Myths about the heart line

<MythReality>
<Myth claim="A broken heart line means heartbreak or divorce.">

No line predicts a break-up. The books that read a broken heart line describe attachment that takes longer to settle: a tendency, not an event.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="A short heart line means you can’t love.">

The books read a short line as feelings shown sparingly. That is about how affection is shown, not how much is felt.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="The heart line shows how healthy your heart is.">

Some old books say this. The app’s rule set leaves out every health claim they make, and so do we: palm lines are not a health test.

</Myth>
</MythReality>
