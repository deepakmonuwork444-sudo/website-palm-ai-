## Does a broken life line mean death or an accident?

No. A break in the life line is not read as death, illness or an accident in any reading we use.

It is a common worry, so the answer comes first. The fear comes from a few old books, not from anything a palm can show.

<KeyPoint>A broken life line is a crease with a gap in it. The books we cite read it as change, never as harm.</KeyPoint>

Some old books did read a break darkly. Cheiro’s *Language of the Hand* (1900) went furthest, above all for a break on both hands.

We name that reading only to set it aside: a crease in the skin can’t foresee an event. Even in 1927, Markun mocked palmists who read years and deaths from this line.

A study tested the life line against real lives. In 2019, Lucas, Dhugga and Henneberg measured the hands of 60 people after death. They found no significant link between the life line’s length and age at death.

More studies are on our page [is palmistry real](/is-palmistry-real/).

### What kind of break is in your life line?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="life-break-clean" title="Clean break in the life line" gist="A time of big change in the way you live." check="Is there a clear gap, with both ends in line with each other?" alt="A life line with a clear gap, both pieces in line" src={['markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Markun (1927) reports the Zancigs’ reading: a break may show “a great change of some sort”. The PalmSays rule set keeps this one reading: a period of major change in the way life is lived. Cheiro’s rule for any broken line is narrow: the break interrupts the line only where it appears.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="life-break-overlap" title="Overlapping break" gist="Change without a real stop: the line’s qualities carry on." check="Does one piece start beside the other, before the first piece ends?" alt="A broken life line where the lower piece starts beside the upper piece, overlapping it" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Cheiro’s rule: if one piece ends above the other, “the break is not so bad and the quality of the line will be continued”. So an overlap is read as a smoother change. A new way of living starts before the old one has ended.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="life-break-square" title="Square over the break" gist="A protective sign, read as support through a time of change." check="Is there a tiny box of four short lines round the gap?" alt="A broken life line with a small square drawn round the gap" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900']}>

Cheiro calls the square the Mark of Preservation, a sign of protection wherever it appears. His *Language of the Hand* says a life line that passes through a square is protected, even where it breaks. The books tie it to escaping danger; we keep only the symbol, support through a hard stretch, not a promise about any event.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="life-break-sister" title="Sister line beside the break" gist="Read as extra backing that carries you past the change." check="Does a second line run just inside your life line, across the gap?" alt="A broken life line with a second, shorter line running just inside it across the gap" src={['markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Markun notes an old rule: a sister line that runs beside a broken line and bridges the gap softens the break. Cheiro says the same of the line of Mars, the inner life line: support beside a break. Their health claims about it are left out.

</Variation>
</Variations>

## Is your life line broken on one hand or both hands?

Check both hands. Markun points out that the lines are not the same on both hands. He also reports an old view: the left hand shows your nature, and the right shows what time and circumstance made of it.

Today’s tradition says it a little differently. The hand you write with is read as the life you are making, and the other as what you started with. So a break on one hand only is read as change on that side of your life.

[Which hand to read](/which-hand-to-read/) explains the traditions in full.

What about a break on both hands? This is where Cheiro’s darkest reading sits, and it is refused here. No line, on one hand or two, can show the end of a life.

## Can a photo make a life line look broken?

Yes. A photo can show a gap where the hand has none. These are the usual causes:

- **A shadow** from a single lamp falls across the line.
- **A fold of skin** forms when the thumb bends in.
- **A crease crosses the line.** Fine lines often run out from the base of the thumb and cut across the life line. Cheiro called these worry lines; others call them influence lines.
- **The line fades** for a short stretch, then comes back. A faint stretch is not a gap.

To check, open your hand flat in bright, even daylight and look from straight above. The [palm photo checker](/tools/palm-photo-checker/) tells you if a photo is clear enough to read. The PalmSays app also waits for a clear trace before it reports a break.

## Broken life line vs a short life line

A break is a gap in the line; the line carries on after it. A short life line simply stops partway round the thumb. Neither one is read as a short life.

## Myths about a broken life line

<MythReality>
<Myth claim="A broken life line means a short life.">

No line can show that. A study that measured life lines found no link between their length and age at death. Even writers in 1927 mocked the idea.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="A break means an accident is coming.">

No mark on the skin can foresee an event. The books we cite read a break as a change in how you live.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="A break on both hands is a death sign.">

That is the darkest old reading, from Cheiro, and it is refused here. Two creases with gaps are still only creases.

</Myth>
