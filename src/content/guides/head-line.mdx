## Head line types and their meanings

<DiagramFigure id="head-line-start-end-and-types" />

Readers look at four things: **how straight or sloping** the line is, **how long** it is, **where it starts** (joined to the life line or not) and **where it ends**. Your head line may fit several cards at once.

<LineAnatomy line="head" />

### Where does your head line start and end?

<CompareRow framed forms={[['head-joined', 'Joined to the life line'], ['head-separate', 'Not joined'], ['head-straight', 'Straight'], ['head-gentle', 'Gently sloping'], ['head-sloping', 'Steeply sloping']]} />

<Variations>
<Variation variant="head-joined" title="Head line joined to the life line" gist="The more common form. The books differ: careful and sensitive, or witty and even-tempered." check="Does your head line start joined to your life line, near the thumb?" alt="A head line that starts joined to the life line before the two separate" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-guide-to-the-hand-1900', 'benham-laws-of-scientific-hand-reading-1900', 'frith-practical-palmistry-1895', 'dale-indian-palmistry-1895']}>

The more common form, and one where the traditions differ. Cheiro and several others read a sensitive, careful mind that weighs other people’s advice before its own, with self-reliance that grows later; Frith adds a shyness often hidden behind a quick manner. Dale’s *Indian Palmistry* reads good wit, an even temper and a sharp practical mind.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-separate" title="Head line not joined to the life line" gist="Independent thinking and quick judgement, with a streak of daring." check="Is there a small gap between the starts of your head and life lines?" alt="A head line that starts a little above the life line, with a gap between them" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'st-hill-grammar-of-palmistry-1893', 'frith-practical-palmistry-1895']}>

Independent thinking and quick judgement, with a streak of mental daring: at its best with a clear purpose to aim at. The books add that this goes with impulsiveness, so second thoughts are often the better ones. A very wide gap is read as boldness that can run ahead of careful thought.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-straight" title="Straight head line" gist="Practical common sense: a mind that prefers the concrete." check="Does your head line run fairly straight across your palm?" alt="A head line running straight across the palm" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'benham-laws-of-scientific-hand-reading-1900', 'cheiro-guide-to-the-hand-1900']}>

Practical common sense: a mind that prefers the concrete and can be relied on to carry a decision through. Several books add settled opinions: once a view is formed, it tends to stay. One reads a line with no curve at all as unyielding, which we take as a caution, not a verdict.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-gentle" title="Gently sloping head line" gist="Imagination kept in hand, with logic and imagination in balance." check="Does your head line slope gently downwards as it crosses?" alt="A head line that slopes gently downwards across the palm" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'cheiro-guide-to-the-hand-1900', 'saint-germain-practice-of-palmistry-1900']}>

Imagination kept in hand: creative thinking used when it is wanted, on a practical footing. The books call it level-headed, with logic and imagination in balance so neither runs the show alone.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-sloping" title="Deeply sloping (curved) head line" gist="A strongly imaginative, idealistic mind, at its best when inspired." check="Does your head line curve steeply down towards the lower outer palm?" alt="A head line that curves steeply down towards the lower outer edge of the palm" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'desbarrolles-chiromancie-nouvelle-1859', 'markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927']}>

A strongly imaginative, idealistic mind that does its best work when inspiration carries it. The books point it towards artistic, literary or intellectual work, and read a line ending low on the outer palm as a pull towards the unusual. Old books attach darker claims to an extreme slope; those are refused here.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-wavy" title="Wavy head line" gist="Attention that comes and goes; decisions that take a while to settle." check="Does your head line wander up and down as it crosses your palm?" alt="A head line that wanders up and down in small waves as it crosses the palm" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900']}>

A head line that wanders up and down as it crosses. Cheiro’s rule for any wavy line is uncertainty and a lack of decision, and *Cheiro’s Language of the Hand* says a wavy line weakens what the line shows. On the head line, that reads as attention that comes and goes, and choices that take a while to settle. It is not a measure of intelligence, and a bent palm can make a line look wavy in a photo.

</Variation>
</Variations>

Cheiro’s books, and Benham for the outer edge, also read where the head line starts and ends. The PalmSays app uses these readings:

- **Starting under the index finger:** ambition joined to judgement, a mind drawn to organising and taking charge.
- **Starting inside the life line**, on the thumb side: a sensitive mind that feels friction with other people keenly.
- **Ending under the middle finger:** depth of thought, with an interest in music or questions of faith.
- **Ending under the ring finger:** a wish to be noticed and known for your ideas.
- **Curving up towards the little finger:** a mind drawn to commerce and science. This is an attitude to money, never a forecast of it.
- **Ending on the outer edge of the palm, fairly level:** practical ideas and the balanced middle course. Cheiro adds strong will and quiet determination when the line starts joined to the life line.

### How long is your head line?

<CompareRow framed forms={[['head-long', 'Long'], ['head-short', 'Short']]} />

<Variations>
<Variation variant="head-long" title="Long head line" gist="Wide mental reach: able to follow big, complex subjects a long way." check="Does your head line run nearly to the outer edge of your palm?" alt="A long head line reaching almost to the outer edge of the palm" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927', 'st-hill-grammar-of-palmistry-1893', 'frith-practical-palmistry-1895']}>

Wide intellectual reach: able to take on large, complex subjects and follow them a long way. When the long line is also unbroken, the books read sound judgement and a steady mind; when it is long and straight, a logical mind that is careful with resources.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-short" title="Short head line" gist="A practical, hands-on mind that focuses closely. A practical, hands-on mind, most at home with concrete tasks and able to focus closely on one specialty. One old book reads a very short line far more darkly; that reading is refused here.

</Variation>
</Variations>

### How clear is your head line?

<CompareRow framed forms={[['head-gentle', 'Deep and clear'], ['head-faint', 'Faint'], ['head-chained', 'Chained']]} />

<Variations>
<Variation variant="head-chained" title="Chained or faint head line" gist="Ideas that shift before they settle, or a mind that ranges widely." check="Up close, is your head line made of tiny links, or thin and faint?" alt="A head line drawn as a row of small links" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

A chained line is read as ideas that shift before they settle, so decisions take longer. A faint line, lying on the surface rather than cut deep, as a mind that ranges more than it concentrates. Neither is a measure of intelligence, and claims about the mind’s health are refused.

</Variation>
</Variations>

A **deep, clearly marked** head line is read as concentration and a retentive memory: thinking that goes deep rather than wide. Dale reads a clear, unbroken line as a cheerful, inventive turn of mind.

### Does your head line break, fork or branch?

<CompareRow framed forms={[['head-fork', 'Forked'], ['head-broken', 'Broken'], ['head-branch-up', 'Branch rising']]} />

<Variations>
<Variation variant="head-fork" title="Forked head line (the writer’s fork)" gist="Two ways of thinking at once: the practical and the imaginative." check="Does your head line split into two branches near its end?" alt="A head line that splits into two branches near its end, one dipping downwards" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916', 'cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'markun-what-you-should-know-about-palmistry-1927', 'benham-laws-of-scientific-hand-reading-1900', 'st-hill-grammar-of-palmistry-1893']}>

Two ways of thinking at once, the practical and the imaginative. That gives range and tact, and can make choosing harder: the old advice is to trust your first impulse. When one branch dips towards the lower outer palm, Cheiro reads a talent for imaginative writing and storytelling, hence the name.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-broken" title="Broken head line" gist="Not a warning: the line’s qualities carry on where the ends overlap." check="Is there a gap in your head line, with the two pieces overlapping?" alt="A head line with a gap where the two ends overlap" src={['cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Cheiro’s rule for any broken line is that the break interrupts what the line shows at that point, and that where the two ends overlap its qualities carry on. So no separate trait is read from the break. Older books attach darker claims; they are refused here.

</Variation>
<Variation variant="head-branch-up" title="Branches from the head line" gist="A branch takes the quality of the mount it reaches: towards the index finger, ambition." check="Does a small line rise from your head line towards one of your fingers?" alt="A head line with a small branch rising towards the index finger" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900']}>

*Cheiro’s Language of the Hand* reads a branch from the head line by where it goes: it takes on the qualities of the mount it runs to. A branch rising towards the index finger (the mount of Jupiter) he reads as pride and ambition to lead; a branch rising to meet the heart line, as a strong attraction or affection that pulls on the mind. A branch dipping towards the lower outer palm is the writer’s fork, above.

</Variation>
</Variations>

## How to tell the head line from the heart line

The head line runs across the middle of your palm, below the [heart line](/heart-line/). To find it and tell the two apart:

<PhotoSteps line="head" />

1. Find the heart line first: the top long line, just under your fingers.
2. Look at the edge of your palm between the thumb and the index finger. The head line starts there, often joined to the [life line](/life-line/) for a short way.
3. Follow it across the palm. It runs fairly straight, or slopes down towards the lower outer edge.

People also call it the **mind line** or **brain line**. In Hindi it is <Hi>मस्तिष्क रेखा</Hi> (mastishk rekha).

**Head line or heart line?** The heart line starts from the little-finger edge and sits higher, just under the fingers. The head line starts from the thumb side and sits lower. If you only see one straight line across the top half of your palm, read about [when the heart and head lines join](#when-the-heart-and-head-lines-join).

<FlowChoice />

### The quadrangle: the space between the head line and heart line

The space between the head line and the heart line has its own name in palmistry: the quadrangle. Cheiro’s *Palmistry for All* (1916) reads it as your attitude towards other people:

- **Even in width**, not narrowing at either end: balance of judgement, a level head.
- **Very narrow:** narrow, fixed views.
- **Very wide:** views so open that judgement can suffer.

On most hands the width changes along the palm, so look at the space as a whole, in even light. The [heart line guide](/heart-line/) explains how Cheiro reads a heart line that sits very low, almost touching the head line.

### Can’t find your head line?

A palm with no head line at all is very rare. Benham writes that he saw one only once, and that the line is present in almost every hand. If you can’t find yours, check for one crease across the palm where the heart and head lines have merged ([the simian line](#when-the-heart-and-head-lines-join)), or for a faint line in low light. The books give no reading for a missing head line, so we don’t invent one.

## Two head lines?

Some palms show a second line running alongside the head line. That is a double head line, which is uncommon and different from a fork. Our guide to the [double head line](/head-line/double/) explains how to tell them apart.

## Education line (vidya rekha)

Some readers today talk of an “education line”, <Hi>विद्या रेखा</Hi> (vidya rekha). The classical books we use don’t name such a line. The English books read thinking style from the head line itself, and Indian texts such as Jain’s *Samudrik Shastra* (1927) link learning to a small barleycorn-shaped mark instead. That mark is far too small for a phone photo. Either way, no line can predict exams, a degree or success.

## Marks on the head line

A good photo shows the head line’s **breaks**, **forks** and **branches**, and the cards above cover those. Islands, chains of tiny loops, dots, crosses, stars and squares are usually only a few pixels wide in a phone photo, so an honest reading leaves them out rather than guessing.

## Head line in the left and right hand, for women and men

The head line is read the same way for everyone; there is no separate head line for women or men. Compare both hands:

- **Your writing hand** is read as the life you are making: how you think now.
- **Your other hand** is read as what you started with.

<WhichHand />

A difference between the two is read as a change in how you think. [Which hand to read](/which-hand-to-read/) explains the traditions in full.

## When the heart and head lines join

Sometimes the heart and head lines form one straight crease across the palm instead of two lines. Palmistry calls it the simian line; doctors call it a single palmar crease, which is usually a normal variation. Benham classes a single line sitting where the head line should be as a head line. The [simian line guide](/simian-line/) gives the medical facts first, then what the books say.

## Myths about the head line

<MythReality>
<Myth claim="A long head line means you are clever, and a short one means you aren’t.">

Length is read as the range of a thinking style, not its power. A short line is read as a focused, practical mind.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="A broken head line means a breakdown or an accident.">

The books that make claims like this are refused. Cheiro’s rule for any broken line is that the qualities carry on where the ends overlap, and a crease or shadow can make a line look broken in a photo.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="The education line shows how far you’ll study.">

The classical books don’t name an education line, and no line can predict exams or degrees.

</Myth>
</MythReality>
