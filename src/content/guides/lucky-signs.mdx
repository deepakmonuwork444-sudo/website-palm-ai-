## What are lucky signs on the palm?

Lucky signs are small, clear marks on the palm that the old palmistry books read kindly. Western books, led by Cheiro, name three: the star, the triangle and the square. Indian books add the fish, the trident and shapes such as the conch, wheel and lotus.

The books rarely use the word “lucky” for a mark. Cheiro calls the star “with one or two exceptions, a fortunate sign” (*Cheiro’s Language of the Hand*, 1900, ch. XVIII). The Indian books call their signs auspicious.

A real sign stands on its own. Cheiro asks that a triangle be clear and distinct, “not formed by the chance crossing of lines” (ch. XXII). The same test works for every sign on this page.

The PalmSays scanner traces the four major lines, not signs, so the photo at the top shows none. Every sign on this page is a **drawing**, in gold, of where the books place it.

## Lucky signs one by one

Each card gives a sign in our own words, from the book named under it. The books also promise riches, fame, children or high rank from many signs. No mark can foretell those, so we leave them out and say so.

### What does a star on the palm mean?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="sign-star-jupiter" title="Star on the mount of Jupiter, under the index finger" gist="Ambition and a wish to lead, read as made stronger." check="Do three or more short lines cross at one point on the pad under your index finger?" alt="A small gold star of three crossing lines on the pad under the index finger" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

A star is three or more short lines crossing at one point. Cheiro reads it as a mark that makes its mount’s quality stronger. Under the index finger that quality is ambition. He also promises honour, power and position here, which no mark can do, so we leave that out.

</Variation>
</Variations>

The same star is read differently on each mount:

| Where the star sits | What Cheiro reads (the part we keep) | What we leave out |
|---|---|---|
| Under the index finger (Jupiter) | Ambition, made stronger | Honour, power and position |
| Under the ring finger (Sun) | A love of art and beauty, made stronger | Riches and glory, which he says bring no contentment |
| Under the little finger (Mercury) | Eloquence and a flair for science or business | “Unusual success” |
| The two mounts of Mars | Patience and fortitude | Honours |
| Outer edge, above the wrist (Moon) | Imagination, made stronger | Fame through it |
| Ball of the thumb (Venus) | Warmth in love | “Extraordinary success” in love |
| Under the middle finger (Saturn) | Nothing: his reading here is a grave forecast | All of it |

Cheiro adds a fair warning of his own. A star, he says, has little power on a hand whose other signs, such as a weak head line, don’t support it.

### What does a triangle on the palm mean?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="sign-triangle-jupiter" title="Triangle on the mount of Jupiter" gist="A talent for managing people and organising everyday affairs." check="Is there a small, clear triangle on the pad under your index finger?" alt="A small gold triangle on the pad under the index finger" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900']}>

Cheiro calls the triangle a curious sign that is often found clear and distinct. Under the index finger he reads more than usual skill in managing people and organising daily affairs.

</Variation>
</Variations>

On the other mounts, *Cheiro’s Language of the Hand* reads the triangle like this:

- **Under the middle finger (Saturn):** a leaning towards mystical study.
- **Under the ring finger (Sun):** putting art to practical use.
- **Under the little finger (Mercury):** a steadier, less restless mind. He also promises success with business or money, which we leave out.
- **On a mount of Mars:** calm in a crisis.
- **On the mount of the Moon:** method in following up the ideas of the imagination.
- **On the mount of Venus:** calm and good sense in love.

### What does a square on the palm mean?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="sign-square-life" title="Square over a break in the life line" gist="The mark of preservation: protection at that point, as a symbol." check="Is there a small, clear box over a gap in one of your lines?" alt="A small gold square drawn over a gap in the life line" src={['cheiro-language-of-the-hand-1900', 'cheiro-palmistry-for-all-1916']}>

Cheiro calls the square “the mark of preservation”. On a line, he reads it as protection at that point from whatever the break seemed to threaten. Read it as a symbol in the books, not a promise. A gap in a line is often a crease or the light, not an event.

</Variation>
</Variations>

On a mount, Cheiro reads a square as keeping that mount’s quality from going too far: ambition under the index finger, or imagination on the mount of the Moon. The [palm mounts](/palm-mounts/) guide explains what each mount is read for.

### What does the fish sign on the palm mean?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="sign-fish" title="Fish at the base of the palm" gist="An auspicious sign in Indian books; Jain reads a religious-minded person." check="Is there a small fish shape near your wrist, at the base of your palm?" alt="A small gold fish shape at the base of the palm, just above the wrist" src={['dale-indian-palmistry-1895', 'jain-samudrik-shastra-1927']}>

The fish is <Hi>मछली</Hi> (machhli), or matsya. Mrs Dale (1895) places it at the root of the hand, and Chhotelal Jain (1927) at the wrist. Jain reads a religious-minded person, and a fish’s tail as the mark of a learned one. Both books also promise riches and children from the fish. No mark can tell either, so we leave that out.

</Variation>
</Variations>

### What does a trident (trishul) on the palm mean?

<Variations>
<Variation variant="sign-trident" title="Trident (trishul)" gist="Jain reads a generous, religious-minded person." check="Do three short lines rise from one stem somewhere on your palm?" alt="A small gold trident, three short lines rising from one stem, under the little finger" src={['jain-samudrik-shastra-1927', 'dale-indian-palmistry-1895']}>

The trident is <Hi>त्रिशूल</Hi> (trishul). Jain reads the sign of a generous, religious-minded person. Mrs Dale reads a rise to fame and high rank, which we leave out. Neither book gives the trident one fixed place, so our drawing shows only its shape.

</Variation>
</Variations>

<KeyPoint>A sign is read as a tendency, never as luck that will arrive. The promises in the old books are theirs, not ours.</KeyPoint>

## Indian auspicious signs: conch, wheel, lotus and flag

Mrs Dale’s *Indian Palmistry* (1895) and Jain’s Hindi book (1927) list more shapes. Most of their readings are promises of wealth, sons or high rank, so only a few tendencies are left to keep:

| Sign | Hindi | What we keep | What we leave out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conch | <Hi>शंख</Hi> (shankh) | Dale: a learned person, above all on the little finger or the mount of Mercury | Nothing else is claimed |
| Flag | <Hi>पताका</Hi> (pataka) | Dale: a philosopher and a great thinker | Jain: a lord of treasure |
| Wheel | <Hi>चक्र</Hi> (chakra) | Jain: with the conch and flag, a learned person who gains standing easily | Riches and power |
| Lotus | <Hi>कमल</Hi> (kamal) | No tendency, only rank | Fame and rank in Dale, a king in Jain |

Jain even ranks the fish, wheel, lotus and conch by the amount of money each brings. That is a money forecast, and no mark can make one. We found no swastika or temple sign in either book, so pages that list them as classical signs give no source.

## Which palm signs do the books not call lucky?

Not every mark is read kindly. Cheiro reads an island as weakening the line it sits on, and a grille as working against the qualities of its mount. He reads most crosses and circles as trouble. We give no meaning for an island at all: the old books read it as illness, and palm lines are not a health test.

The best-known exception is the mystic cross between the heart and head lines, read as an interest in the spiritual. The [crosses on the palm](/palm-crosses/) guide covers it and the other crosses.

## Are lucky signs rare?

We can’t say, and we won’t guess. No book we use counts how often any sign appears, and we know of no study that does. So we call none of them rare.

What makes signs hard to find is their size and the light. A sign is small, and many apparent signs are short lines that cross by chance. Check yours in bright daylight, from more than one angle.

## Can a phone photo show these signs?

Usually not. Stars, triangles, squares and fish are only a few pixels wide in most phone photos, too small to see reliably. The PalmSays app observes marks like these but leaves them out of the reading for that reason. Look at your own hand instead.

## Lucky signs in the left and right hand

A sign is read the same way on either hand, for women and men alike. Tradition reads your writing hand as the life you are making and the other as what you started with. Jain lists separate signs for women, nearly all about husbands and sons; we leave those out. The guide to [which hand to read](/which-hand-to-read/) explains the traditions.

## Myths about lucky signs

<MythReality>
<Myth claim="A fish on your palm means wealth.">

The Indian books do promise it, but no mark on the skin can foretell money. What we keep from Jain is a religious-minded nature, and learning for a fish’s tail.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="A star on the palm is always lucky.">

Cheiro himself made exceptions. He read a star under the middle finger darkly, and said a star means little when the rest of the hand doesn’t support it.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="Rare signs mark a special fate.">

No book we use counts how rare any sign is. A sign is read as a leaning in character, and your choices matter more than any mark.

</Myth>
</MythReality>
