## How to tell your hand type

Your hand type comes from two things: the shape of your palm and the length of your fingers. Palmistry calls the study of the hand’s shape chirognomy, the half of palm reading that comes before the lines.

1. **Measure your palm.** Its length runs from the crease at your wrist to the base of your middle finger. Its width runs across the knuckles, below the fingers. If the two are about equal, your palm is square; if the length is clearly greater, it is long.
2. **Measure your fingers.** Compare your middle finger with your palm’s length. Clearly shorter means short fingers; nearly as long means long fingers.
3. **Look at the tips and joints.** Square, rounded, pointed or widening fingertips, and smooth or knotty joints, point to the classical types below.

Our hand type tool uses this rule of thumb: a palm up to 1.15 times as long as it is wide counts as square, 1.25 or more as long, and anything between is “between two types”. Markun (1927) describes an older way to measure: the middle finger from its tip to the knuckle on the back of the hand, the palm from the base of that finger to the wrist.

## The four element hands (a modern system)

The earth, air, fire and water hands are a **modern** way of sorting hands, popular in 20th-century palmistry books. The classical books we cite don’t use it, so we don’t say “the tradition says” about it. It rests on just the two measurements above.

### Earth hand

A square palm with short fingers. In this modern system an earth hand is linked with a practical, steady nature: someone who likes doing things with their hands and trusts what is concrete.

### Air hand

A square palm with long fingers. An air hand is linked with a curious, talkative mind, at home with ideas, words and other people.

### Fire hand

A long palm with short fingers. A fire hand is linked with energy and enthusiasm: quick to start things and drawn to action.

### Water hand

A long palm with long fingers. A water hand is linked with sensitivity and imagination, tuned in to feelings and moods.

## Cheiro’s seven hand types (the classical system)

The older system has seven types. The French writer Casimir d’Arpentigny set them out, and Edward Heron-Allen’s 1886 translation gives his names: the elementary (large-palmed), the necessary (spatulate), the artistic (conic), the useful (square), the philosophic (knotted), the psychic (pointed) and the mixed hand. Cheiro’s *Palmistry for All* (1916) follows the same seven.

<HandShapes />

Not every writer agreed. Adolphe Desbarrolles, quoted in Heron-Allen’s notes, thought the classification useless: hands may resemble one another, he said, but nature never repeats itself. So read a type as a rough guide, not a box.

### Elementary hand

A short, thick-set palm with short fingers and a short thumb. Cheiro judges this type harshly, in words we won’t repeat; we keep only the shape.

### Square hand

A palm that looks square, straight at the wrist and at the base of the fingers, with square-cut fingertips and a long thumb. Cheiro calls it the practical or useful hand: logical, methodical and painstaking, believing things on proof.

### Spatulate hand

Fingertips that widen at the ends, like a spatula, on an irregular palm. Cheiro reads untiring energy, originality and a love of invention. He adds that a palm widest at the base of the fingers is more practical, and one widest at the wrist more impulsive.

### Philosophic hand

A long, bony, angular hand with knotty finger joints. Cheiro reads a studious, thoughtful nature, a great reader, and says the knotted joints give carefulness and a love of detail.

### Conic hand

A graceful hand with tapering, rounded fingers, which Cheiro calls the artistic hand. He reads an artistic temperament that loves beautiful surroundings and is sensitive to colour and music, and says the firmer the hand, the more its owner tends to make of that gift.

### Psychic hand

A long, narrow hand with slender, pointed fingers, which Cheiro calls the idealistic hand. He reads a nature that lives among ideals and dreams more than practical matters. His other remarks about this type are dated and bleak, and we leave them out.

### Mixed hand

Fingers of different types on one hand, such as one pointed, one square and one spatulate, or a palm of one type with mixed fingers. Cheiro reads versatility: someone who can turn to many things and talk on almost any subject.

## What if your hand fits more than one type?

Many hands don’t match one picture exactly, and the books expect that. Cheiro’s *Language of the Hand* (1900) describes a square palm with long, short, knotty, spatulate, conic or pointed fingers, each read a little differently. In *Palmistry for All* he notes that a square palm with philosophic fingers is more common than the pure philosophic hand.

So read the palm and the fingers separately:

- **The palm** gives the base: square is read as practical, long as more imaginative.
- **The fingers** add the detail. Cheiro reads long fingers as a love of detail and short fingers as quick and impulsive, taking things in as a whole. The [fingers in palmistry](/palmistry-fingers/) guide covers length, joints, gaps and the thumb.

If a measurement sits right between two types, say so. That is an honest answer, not a failed reading.

## How hand type fits the rest of a palm reading

Hand shape is the first thing a reader looks at, before the lines. Cheiro reads the lines against the type of hand: in *Language of the Hand* he says a sun line is more heavily marked on philosophic, conic and psychic hands, and means less there than the same line on a square or spatulate hand. The [palm mounts](/palm-mounts/) are read with the shape too.

<KeyPoint>Shape first, then the lines: a type is the frame the rest of the reading sits in, not a verdict on its own.</KeyPoint>

## Hand types in the left and right hand, for women and men

Hand types are read the same way for everyone; there is no separate type for women or men. Your two hands are usually the same type, but compare them. Cheiro notes that work and exercise may enlarge or broaden a hand, but its type stays recognisable. [Which hand to read](/which-hand-to-read/) explains why tradition reads the two hands differently.

## Myths about hand types

<MythReality>
<Myth claim="Earth, air, fire and water hands come from ancient palmistry.">

They are a modern system. The classical books use seven types, from the elementary hand to the mixed hand.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="Your hand type decides your career.">

The books link types with tendencies, such as a practical or an imaginative bent. Even Cheiro says the conic hand’s gift depends on the effort its owner makes.

</Myth>
<Myth claim="Every hand fits exactly one type.">

Many hands are mixed or sit between two types, and the books describe these combinations on purpose.

</Myth>
