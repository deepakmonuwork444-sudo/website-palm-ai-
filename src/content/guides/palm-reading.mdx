## What is palm reading?

Palm reading, or palmistry, is the old practice of reading the lines, mounts and shape of the hand. In India it is called <Hi>हस्त रेखा शास्त्र</Hi> (Hast Rekha Shastra). It is often described as part of <Hi>सामुद्रिक शास्त्र</Hi> (Samudrika Shastra), the study of the body’s features.

A reader looks at three things: the four major lines, the raised pads called mounts (<Hi>पर्वत</Hi>, parvat), and small marks such as breaks and branches. The books link each of these with a side of character: how you feel, think, meet change and choose.

Nothing in a palm can tell what will happen. Traditional readings work best as prompts to think about yourself, and that is how this guide uses them. Every meaning below comes from a named book, in our own words.

## What you need to read a palm

You need good light, both hands and a few minutes. Daylight near a window works best. A single lamp throws shadows that can look like extra lines or breaks. Hold your hand flat and open, with the whole palm in view.

A photo helps, because you can zoom in and compare your two hands side by side. Take one photo of each palm, straight from above, with the crease at your wrist in the frame. PalmSays has a palm photo checker that tells you whether a photo is clear enough to read.

You don’t need a magnifying glass or any special kit. You don’t need to know the terms yet either: each step names what to look for.

## Step 1: Choose which hand to read

Start by choosing which hand to read, because palmistry treats your two hands differently. Tradition reads the hand you write with as the life you are making, and the other as what you started with. *Cheiro’s Language of the Hand* (1900) puts it this way: “The left is the hand we are born with; the right is the hand we make.” Linking this to your writing hand, left or right, is a later convention.

So read both hands, and notice where they differ. If you are left-handed, your left hand is your writing hand. [Which hand to read](/which-hand-to-read/) explains the traditions in full.

## Step 2: Find your hand shape

Before the lines, look at the shape of your whole hand: the palm, and the length and tips of the fingers. Cheiro’s *Palmistry for All* (1916) sorts hands into seven types:

- **Elementary:** a short, thick-set hand with short fingers.
- **Square:** a palm that looks square, with square-cut fingers. Cheiro calls it the practical or useful hand.
- **Spatulate:** fingertips that widen at the ends, like a spatula. He links it with energy and originality.
- **Philosophic:** a long, bony hand with knotty joints, linked with a studious, thoughtful nature.
- **Conic:** a graceful hand with tapering fingers, which he calls the artistic hand.
- **Psychic:** a long, narrow hand with slender, tapering fingers, which he calls the idealistic hand.
- **Mixed:** fingers of different types on one hand, linked with versatility.

Cheiro judges some of these types harshly. We keep only the shapes and the traits most people would recognise. Many modern websites sort hands into earth, air, fire and water types instead. That system is newer and isn’t in the old books we use. The PalmSays app doesn’t read hand shape, so this step is one you do by eye.

## Step 3: Read the heart line

The heart line is the highest long line across your palm, just below the fingers. It starts at the little-finger edge and runs towards the index or middle finger. In Hindi it is <Hi>हृदय रेखा</Hi> (hriday rekha). Palmistry reads it for your emotional life: how you feel, show affection and relate to people.

Three common forms to look for:

- **Ending under the index finger:** Cheiro and Markun read loyal, steady affection held to high ideals.
- **Short:** feelings shown sparingly, not always put into words. That describes how feeling is shown, not how much is felt.
- **Forked at the end:** a balanced, faithful style of affection, with ideals and everyday warmth held together.

Books disagree about which end of this line to read from, so a careful reading shows both views. The [heart line guide](/heart-line/) shows every type with its source.

## Step 4: Read the head line

The head line runs across the middle of the palm, below the heart line. It often starts joined to the life line, near the thumb, and then separates. In Hindi it is <Hi>मस्तिष्क रेखा</Hi> (mastishk rekha). Palmistry reads it for how you think and decide.

Look at its shape first:

- **Straight:** Cheiro reads practical common sense, a mind that prefers the concrete.
- **Gently curved:** imagination kept in hand, creative thinking used on a practical footing. A line that slopes steeply is read as a strongly imaginative mind.
- **Forked at the end:** two ways of thinking at once, practical and imaginative, which gives range but can make choosing harder.

Also note whether it starts joined to the life line. The books call that the more common form, and read a careful, cautious mind. The [head line guide](/head-line/) covers every type, and two separate head lines have [their own page](/head-line/double/).

## Step 5: Read the life line

The life line curves around the base of the thumb. It starts between the thumb and index finger and sweeps towards the wrist. In Hindi it is <Hi>जीवन रेखा</Hi> (jeevan rekha). Palmistry reads it for vitality, stability and how you meet change.

Its length is **not** read as how long you will live. Even the old books warn against reading it as a count of years. Look at these forms instead:

- **A wide curve** out into the palm: an outgoing, energetic presence, energy that reaches out towards people.
- **A line that stays close to the thumb:** energy held closer in, a quieter presence.
- **A break:** a period of major change in the way life is lived, not illness or harm.

The old books also read the life line for health. The app’s rules leave that out, and so do we. The [life line guide](/life-line/) explains each type, including the short life line.

## Step 6: Read the fate line

The fate line runs up the middle of the palm towards the middle finger. In Hindi it is <Hi>भाग्य रेखा</Hi> (bhagya rekha), and in English it is also called the destiny or career line. Palmistry reads it for direction, work and a sense of purpose.

Many palms show a faint fate line or none at all. That is common. The books read a missing fate line as a path that follows choices as they come, not one fixed track. Where you do see one, look at where it starts:

- **At the wrist:** Markun reads responsibility that arrives early, a sense of duty towards work that starts young.
- **On the outer side of the palm** (the Moon mount): a path shaped a great deal by other people, where the public or partners play a large part.
- **Broken, or changing course:** a working life with a real change of direction, rather than one straight road.

## Step 7: Read the mounts and signs

The mounts are the soft, raised pads on your palm. Indian palmistry calls them <Hi>पर्वत</Hi> (parvat) and links each one with a planet. Press gently around your palm and notice which pads feel full and which feel flat. The books read a well-developed mount like this:

- **Guru (Jupiter)**, under the index finger: ambition and a wish to lead.
- **Shani (Saturn)**, under the middle finger: seriousness and responsibility.
- **Surya (Sun)**, under the ring finger: creativity and a love of beauty.
- **Budh (Mercury)**, under the little finger: communication and a quick wit.
- **Shukra (Venus)**, the large pad at the base of the thumb: warmth and sympathy.
- **Chandra (Moon)**, on the outer edge of the palm, above the wrist: imagination.
- **Mangal (Mars)** has two mounts. The one near the thumb, just inside the start of the life line, is read for active courage. The one on the outer edge, above the Moon mount, is read for moral courage and self-control.

Signs are the small marks on and between the lines: islands, crosses, stars, squares and triangles. The books give each a meaning, but most are too small for a phone photo to show reliably. An honest reading leaves them out. Breaks, forks and branches, on the other hand, show up well.

Smaller lines, such as the [marriage line](/marriage-line/) on the edge of the hand, come after the four major ones. The [guide to all the palm lines](/hand-lines/) maps them.

## Palm reading for a woman or a man

The lines are read the same way for everyone. There is no separate heart line or life line for women and men, and no line reads a woman’s or a man’s fate. What traditions differ on is which hand to read.

Dale’s *Indian Palmistry* (1895) records an Eastern custom of reading a man’s right palm and a woman’s left. It then advises reading the hand where the lines show most clearly. In this guide we follow the writing-hand tradition for everyone. [Which hand to read](/which-hand-to-read/) covers female and male palm reading in full.

## Common beginner mistakes

1. **Reading finger creases as palm lines.** The short creases at the base of each finger are finger creases. The heart line is the first long line below them.
2. **Mixing up the heart and head lines.** The heart line starts at the little-finger edge. The head line below it usually starts near the thumb, often joined to the life line.
3. **Reading only one hand.** Tradition compares both, and a line that looks different on each hand is part of the reading.
4. **Taking shadows for breaks.** Side light, a bent palm or a blurry photo can make a line look broken, forked or doubled. Check in even daylight before you read a break.
5. **Reading length as lifespan.** A short life line is not a short life. Palm lines are not a health test either.
6. **Treating a reading as a prediction.** Palmistry describes tendencies. A reading that gives a date, an illness or a number of children has gone beyond anything a palm can show.

## Questions palmistry can’t answer

Palmistry can’t tell you when something will happen or how long you will live. It can’t say whether you are healthy, when or whom you will marry, how many children you will have, or how much money you will make. Palm lines are skin creases that form before birth, and there is no scientific support for the idea that they predict events. For what research actually says, see [whether palmistry is real](/is-palmistry-real/).

What palm reading can offer is a way to reflect: to notice how you tend to feel, think and choose, and to talk about it.
